# Worked example — ad gets clicks but no form fills, what do I check?

A real Q&A session showing how this brain loads, cites the experts by name, and lands
the user on the pre-flight checklist. Quotes here are verbatim from the mined transcripts; see
`references/quote-library.md` for attribution.

---

**User:** My Meta ad is getting loads of clicks and a decent CTR, but almost nobody
fills in the form on the landing page. Cost per lead is climbing. What do I check?

**Brain response:**

Healthy clicks with poor conversions is a congruence problem, not a targeting problem.
Do not touch the audience yet. As Joe Sayles, Jr. puts it, "strong clicks with poor
conversions is the symptom of a disconnect between the ad and the landing page." Nick
Theriot is blunt on why the test is even worth running: "If there's no congruence
between the ad and the landing page, it won't yield a proper test."

Work through it in this order.

**1. Where does the ad point?**
If it points at your homepage or any page with a nav bar, that is the first leak. A
homepage restarts the pitch and gives the visitor exits. Send ad traffic to a
dedicated, single-focus page instead. Theriot's mechanism for why this works: "they
can't go anywhere else. They're walled off. And you have completion bias right here...
people when they start something, they have a high likelihood of finishing it."

**2. Does the page continue the ad's specific angle, or restart it?**
The most common silent killer is a page that sells from scratch instead of picking up
the ad's thread. Build the page FROM the winning ad, in the ad's own order. Scalability
School describe the move literally: "we just like took the transcript of the ad, looked
at who was responding to the ad... and then said, okay, what is this ad saying?" The
page's job is not to close in one line, it is to keep the argument going. Theriot again:
"That's the difference there with landing page. It just offers more information and more
repetition and repetition creates more belief."

**3. Does the headline match the ad word-for-word?**
Sayles reduces this to one question: "Headline match. Does your landing page headline
match your ad headline word for word?" If the ad promised a specific benefit and the
page opens on a different line, the visitor's scent breaks and they bounce.

**4. Do the people shown on the page match who the ad targets?**
Congruence is persona-level, not just words. Replo's case: an ad shows "two men as the
hero. Um, but it's actually targeted towards women over the age of 45." If the first
face on the page is the wrong buyer, the match is broken even when the copy is right.

**5. Is the above-the-fold promise honest and instant?**
Oliver Kenyon flags the trust break where the page claims one thing at the top and
delivers another lower down: "we've we've seen from a lot of uh data analysis and
customer feedback that people won't purchase because you say 2,300 up here, but when you
actually get to it, it's only 10 and something."

**6. Is the page fast on mobile?**
A slow page reads as a relevance failure. Mustafa Afridi's threshold: "as long as this
is below 3 seconds, you are in a good place," and "most of your traffic on Google is
going to come from mobile" — the same holds on Meta.

---

**Land on the pre-flight checklist.** Before this ad points at this page again, every
line must be true:

1. Headline on the page matches the ad's headline/promise word-for-word.
2. First paragraph names the same benefit the ad promised.
3. Same angle/pain-point continued, not restarted.
4. Same look: colours, fonts, hero image style match the ad.
5. Persona match: the people shown match who the ad targets (age, gender, situation).
6. Single focus: one action, no nav bar, no competing links.
7. Loads in under 3 seconds on mobile.
8. It is a dedicated page for this angle, not the homepage.

Fix the failing lines first, then re-judge the ad. If you want a fast sanity pass,
McGarry's audit tactic: "You can feed your page in, feed your copy in... and ask it if
there is narrative congruence."

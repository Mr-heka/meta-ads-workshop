# Primal Video Editing Method: The 9-Step Process and Signature Techniques

The full editing process Justin Brown teaches at Primal Video, step by step, with verbatim quotes. Source transcripts are not included in this kit; `source-index.md` lists titles and URLs. See `source-index.md` for the full source list.

---

## The central thesis

> "The most efficient way to edit and to learn to edit is by following a specific process so that you know that you're doing the right things in the right order and not unnecessarily wasting your time." (video 01)

> "Most people that are doing this are either treating this as one big project, which becomes overwhelming, or they're jumping into the wrong things first. Too many people jump in and try to make their videos look good first instead of doing what they actually need to do. So they end up jumping all over the place, adding to their frustration and burning a lot of time." (video 02)

The Primal Video Method is "literally step 1 do this, step 2 do this" (video 03). The method is tool-agnostic. Justin demonstrates it across CapCut, VN Video Editor, Shotcut, and Descript and stresses: "I'm showing you the step-by-step process here to edit, you can actually follow along and follow the same processes in whatever editing tool or software you'd like to use" (video 01).

Three principles run through every step:

1. **Iterative passes.** "Editing is an iterative process so we'll do a pass, we'll clean things up, tidy it up a bit, and there'll be further refining on the next pass." (video 01) "We're going to make minor changes and stuff as we go through. And then we'll do another pass and go through and refine it further." (video 02)
2. **Content / story first.** "We're focusing on the story first." (video 02) "You really should be first and foremost focusing on the content first." (video 03)
3. **Polish last.** "A lot of people get [this] wrong and they do [it] first up: they want to make their video look pretty, look really nice, add all these effects and things on before they've cut it down, and all that's going to do is slow down your computer and make the editing process more frustrating." (video 01)

## Step 1: Set up the project

- Create a new project. Set the **format / aspect ratio**: 16:9 widescreen, or 9:16 / portrait for a short or TikTok video.
- Let the software auto-detect resolution, frame rate, and color space. The key mechanic: "this is where a lot of these settings are going to be pre-populated or automatically generated from the first video clip that you bring into the project, so as long as you're aware of that and the first video clip you bring into your project is your main camera footage, then you can actually let your software decide." (video 01)
- If you do want manual control: edit at the same quality you filmed at. "Ideally here you're editing in the same quality that you're filming at, so if your video recording was at 1080p 25 frames per second then you would pick that here to keep the quality as high as it can be." (video 04)
- Delete any auto-added end card or watermark: "tools like VN Video Editor and CapCut and some others, they'll automatically add a little title card or a watermark right at the end of the video. So I'm just scrolling across here, we can remove this." (video 02)
- Interface orientation: media library top-left, playback/preview window, and the **timeline** at the bottom. "Down the bottom here we have our timeline, this is where the magic happens, this is where you will be actually performing your edits." (video 01)

## Step 2: Import and lay down primary footage

- Import the **primary camera footage first**, "our primary footage, the thing that's going to make up the bulk of our edit" (video 02). Importing it first also lets the project auto-configure off it (Step 1).
- A clip is not editable until it is in the timeline. "We're not ready to start editing it until it's down in our timeline." (video 01)
- You can drag the whole clip down, or mark in/out points to bring only a section. For one big interview clip, marking the section first keeps it "much more manageable" (video 01).
- Multiple clips: "bring them all down and stack them one after the other to build out the overall sequence of your edit." (video 01)
- Use timeline zoom: "if we zoom in here you can see that we're going to see more details which means we can make more precise edits." (video 01)

## Step 3: Trim down: remove bad takes and mistakes

The first real editing pass. "Remove all the bad takes, all the mistakes, all the things that we actually don't want to have in our finished project." (video 01)

- **Trim by the waveform.** "A great way that I like to do this, especially if it's a spoken piece like this, is to look at the audio waveforms ... we've got the visual representation of what's being said or where there's sound in there." (video 02) The waveform shows "the areas where I'm speaking, where I'm not speaking, and we can actually use those to help us edit down faster" (video 01).
- Trim the start: drag the clip's left edge in to where speech begins. Trim the end: drag the right edge back.
- Remove mid-clip gaps: move the playhead, use the **blade / split tool** (scissors; shortcut Command/Control B in CapCut, S in Shotcut and Descript) to split, select the dead section, delete it.
- After the pass you are left with "a bunch of little clips", "chunks of footage that we can just click on, we can drag, we can swap the order around, we can pick these pieces up to build out the edit that we want" (video 01).

## Step 4: Add b-roll / overlay footage

- Import b-roll, screen recordings, graphics, "anything in addition to your primary footage that you want to show on screen" (video 02).
- Drop b-roll onto a layer **above** the primary footage so it covers the picture while the primary audio keeps playing: "we actually drop it on top of our primary footage so that it sits above it ... so we're still hearing everything, we're just not seeing it." (video 01)
- Mark in/out on long b-roll clips before placing them.
- **Mute b-roll audio** unless you specifically want it: "let's just mute the audio on this top b-roll footage layer." (video 01)
- Justin's b-roll/stock sources: Storyblocks, plus stock libraries built into Descript, Canva, and Placeit.
- In Descript b-roll is placed by transcript: put a slash before and after the sentence to create a "scene", then drag stock footage onto that sentence (video 05).

## Step 5: Add text and titles

- Add names, lower-thirds, subscribe prompts. A title behaves like a clip: "it comes in as if it's its own video clip that we can adjust the start time, the end time, all of that stuff." (video 01)
- Customise font, size, weight, character spacing, position. Match it to the brand.
- Animations: simple in/out animations are fine; templates exist for more advanced looks (subscribe badges, etc.).
- For multi-line titles, split lines into separate text layers so each can be positioned independently (video 04).

## Step 6: Add transitions and effects

- **Transitions** sit between two clips. **Effects** sit on a clip.
- Effects include speed up / slow down, stabilisation, noise reduction, image enhancement, color effects. "The fancy stuff happens at this point." (video 02)
- Transition discipline (see the over-editing philosophy below): only between genuinely different footage. Between two similar talking-head shots, use the fake second-angle zoom instead (see Signature Techniques).

## Step 7: Add music and sound effects

- **Licensing first.** Do not use a tool's built-in music unless you are certain of the license: "I would be skeptical about using them unless you know that you will be 100% compliant uploading them to places like YouTube. You don't want to have some sort of copyright issue." (video 02)
- Justin's music sources: **Artlist** and **Epidemic Sound**.
- Music goes on its own audio track (usually below the video tracks). It is a clip like any other, trim it so it ends when the video ends.
- Match the edit to the music: "we're making any minor adjustments to our edits here to maybe line up some of the edits or cuts to the beat so that it flows the way that we'd like." (video 01)
- Crossfade between two music tracks by overlapping them; add a fade-out filter at the end.

## Step 8: Adjust volume levels

- **Spoken word first, music and SFX second.** "We want to get the audio volume levels right on our spoken piece first because that's the important piece, and then we'll adjust our music and sound effects and things afterwards." (video 01)
- Mute the music while you set the spoken levels.
- Use audio meters / bars. The target: "we want to make sure that our audio is in the green, into the yellow or orange, but really not into the red." Hitting the zero mark means "our audio would be too loud and distorting, and we definitely don't want to go over that." (video 01)
- Set levels on the first clip, then copy/paste or apply across all clips.
- **Music starting point: around -25 to -30 dB.** "Where I like to start with this is somewhere between -25 and -30." (video 01) "I like to start around 30 or minus 30 here." (video 02) Adjust per track.
- Always check **with headphones**, and play back on a second device: "play it back on another device so that you've got a real representation of what it's going to sound like for viewers." (video 02)
- In Descript: music is auto-ducked (about -12 dB under speech), and **volume keyframes** let you dip music under speech and lift it under b-roll (video 05).

## Step 9: Color correct, then export

Color is the **last creative step**, deliberately. Doing it earlier "all it ends up doing is slowing it down because your computer or your phone is having to process that effect or those adjustments every time you play the video. That's why you save this towards the end." (video 02)

- Start on the **first clip**. Optionally use filters (preset looks), or adjust manually.
- Justin's manual order: **brightness/exposure -> contrast -> white balance / temperature -> saturation**. (video 02 reorders to brightness -> temperature -> contrast -> saturation; the set of four is the constant.)
- "You don't want to go too far with this stuff. Minor adjustments." (video 01)
- Apply to all clips, then color the b-roll separately.
- Advanced tools (Shotcut) expose color wheels for shadows/midtones/highlights and a white-balance picker, "the trick here while you're tweaking your colors is not to move it around too much." (video 04)
- **Export:** use a platform preset (YouTube, TikTok) or the project's native settings. Most tools auto-detect the right export settings from the footage. For quality, bump bit rate up if needed. After export, "play back the video on your computer or on your device that you edited it on, but I'd also try to play it back on another device." (video 02)

## Signature techniques

### Edit backwards (the "game changer")

The single highest-leverage technique for talking-head footage.

The filming half: when presenting to camera, "I will only ever move on to the next dot point, the next paragraph, the next section of the video when I am happy with the last take." (video 03) This guarantees the **last take of every section is the keeper**.

The editing half: because the keeper is always last, edit from the END of the timeline backwards. "Instead of editing from left to right or start to finish, you're actually able to edit from the back ... that last thing that you have said is the one that you want to use, then you're going backwards through and you're hitting those best takes first, so it's much much faster for you to edit." (video 03)

> "When I'm presenting and I stumble or make a mistake I will redo that section or that chunk, so that I know when I'm filming I'm only moving on when I'm happy with the last take. So knowing that the last take is the one I want to use, then I'm going to edit backwards so that I'm hitting that take straight away and I'm not needing to play through all the different variations or attempts." (video 01)

> "This tip is an absolute game changer and it will totally change the way you are creating your content." (video 03)

**Exception:** with transcript-based editors (Descript), edit **forward**. The AI retake-removal already strips bad takes, and you can see repeated lines in the transcript and delete them directly: "between the AI tool taking out the bad takes theoretically and the fact that you can see the transcript on the screen I find editing forward is easiest." (video 05)

### Filming markers for yourself

While filming, leave yourself audio/visual notes for the edit: put a hand to the lens, or clap to create an audio spike in the waveform "something that's going to grab your own attention when you're editing your videos down" (video 03). Saves taking written notes mid-shoot.

### The fake second-camera-angle cut

Two near-identical talking-head clips produce a jarring jump cut. Justin's fix is NOT a transition:

> "To smooth that hard cut out, what I would normally do is just pick either the first or the second clip ... and I would zoom in on one of the clips to make it look like it was a closer up shot or to change things up enough so that it almost feels like a second camera angle." (video 01)

Critical details:
- **Keep the eye-line constant.** "In order to really sell this effect, if you make sure the person's eyes are in a very similar position it's going to be way less jarring." (video 01)
- **Do not zoom too far** or you lose quality: "depending on how you've shot this and your camera quality, you don't want to go too far because you could be losing quality here." (video 01)
- This is the Primal Video default "instead of just having a bunch of transitions every couple of seconds." (video 01)
- Descript calls the same move a **zoom cut** and lets you save it as a one-click template so "the zoom cut goes in the same amount every time." (video 05)

### Set effects on the first clip, then apply to all

For both volume (Step 8) and color (Step 9): dial the setting in on the first clip, confirm it, then "apply to all" or copy/paste the effect across the timeline. Then handle b-roll separately. "I always like to apply these things on the first clip and then we can copy and paste them or apply them to the rest as we need to." (video 02)

### Save backup versions of the timeline

Beginner mistake #8. Do not work one timeline start to finish with no way back.

> "As you progress through, every now and then, or every time you hit a milestone, or ideally every time you're moving into one of the next steps inside of our Primal Video Method editing process, [save a version] ... then you've got the ability to quickly go back and find stuff or to open up a previous version of your project if you need to, if something bad happens, if the project gets corrupted." (video 03)

### Keyboard shortcuts

Beginner mistake #5. "Learning and understanding the keyboard shortcuts are going to speed up your editing tenfold." (video 03) The ones Justin names: **J, K, L** for playback (back/stop/forward), **ripple edit left / ripple edit right** (also called trim top / trim tail), **split** (S in Shotcut and Descript, Command/Control B in CapCut), **spacebar** for play/pause.

## The no-over-editing philosophy

Justin's strongest review rule. Over-editing is beginner mistake #7.

> "Over editing your videos: making way too many cuts, removing out every little pause or every little breath, just adding cuts for the sake of adding cuts, or going the other way and adding in way too many transitions and too many effects, and all that does is annoy the viewers and make it distracting and hard for them to keep watching your content. If anything stick to simple cuts, remove the mistakes, tighten up stuff if it needs it. For the most part the more you simplify your videos the easier they're going to be for you to edit, but also the easier for your viewers to watch without them just being overwhelmed and distracted by a whole lot of unnecessary noise or distractions." (video 03)

Transition rule: a transition is for change, not sameness. "These work well if we're transitioning from one clip or one style of footage to something totally different, but in the case where it's almost the same I wouldn't apply one." (video 01) Between two similar talking-head shots: leave the hard cut, or use the fake second-angle zoom.

For YouTube specifically Justin keeps it bare: "I generally don't add a lot of transitions for my YouTube videos, they're pretty much all basic jump cuts." (video 05) He wants "the edits to be relatively tight" (video 05).

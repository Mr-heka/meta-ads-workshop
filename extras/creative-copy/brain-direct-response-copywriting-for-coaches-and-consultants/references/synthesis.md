# Synthesis — direct response copywriting for coaches and consultants

Clustered by theme. Under each: the point, which experts back it, and the
concrete number or example they gave. Consensus and contested are marked.

---

## 1. The lead is the leverage point

**CONSENSUS.** "Lead" means the first 2–3 pages of a sales letter or the first
1–2 minutes of a VSL — not a sales prospect.

Stefan Georgi rewrote only the lead of a mediocre V-Shred offer and beat the
control by 72%, taking it from roughly 10–20 sales a day to about 1,500. He was
paid $63,000 for a single two-page lead rewrite, under a deal paying $3,000 per
percentage point of lift; of three competing leads he wrote for that client, one
lost, one was flat, and one beat control by 21%. His co-host Justin runs a
comparable deal at $10,000 up front plus $25,000 on a 25% lift, and calls lead
rewrites "super super low-hanging fruit" because the work itself takes an hour or
two.

Georgi's stated priority: "I would 100% test new leads before new big ideas" —
the effort-to-payoff ratio is better, because a new lead takes days and a genuinely
new big idea takes far longer.

Roy Furr supplies the vocabulary the rest of the corpus lacks. A **hook** is an
idea — a curiosity-provoking angle, often a micro example that zooms out to a
macro opportunity, or a parable about someone who had the problem and solved it. A
**headline** is copy, and its only job is to "buy readership." A **lead** is copy
that pulls the reader deeper while withholding the payoff. Furr explicitly
contrasts this with Sherlock Holmes structure, where the reveal comes at the end —
that is the opposite of good direct response.

### Georgi's five lead mistakes, with fixes

1. **Burying the lead / throat clearing.** Filler, disclaimers and scene-setting
   before the hook. On a real ClickBank example, cutting the first five or six
   sentences — all preamble about the US election and not being "for or against"
   anyone — would in his estimate produce "a pretty huge conversion boost."
2. **Leaning on broad statistics.** General stats appeal to the brain, not the
   heart, and break the illusion of a one-to-one conversation. Contrast: an acid
   reflux lead stacking "1,345% more likely to get cancer" claims against a
   zero-statistic, all-story BioHarmony lead that was outperforming it
   commercially.
3. **Ron Burgundy syndrome.** First-person "I" story for paragraph after paragraph
   with no relation back to the reader. Fix: address "you" early and often; even in
   third-person stories, invite the reader in with "imagine you're…".
4. **Being a teacher instead of a marketer.** Mechanism names, biology, citations.
   Keep it "battery enzyme," not "NP241." Hint at the surprising cause; save the
   explanation for later, once curiosity is banked.
5. **Forgetting to fascinate.** Stack 3–5 curiosity bullets near the end of the
   lead, each posing an unanswered riddle. Source material Georgi names: the
   *Screaming Eagle* newsletter, which contains roughly 21 distinct categories of
   curiosity bullet. He calls studying it one of the three-to-five things that
   improved his copy most.

**Bonus pitfall.** Opening with stacked "Do you want X? Are you ready for Y?"
questions reads as a pitch and puts the reader on guard. Georgi notes Craig Clemens
used question leads successfully in the Double Your Dating letters, but avoids them
himself — a genuine stylistic disagreement, not a settled rule.

## 2. One idea, ruthlessly

**CONSENSUS, and the most heavily evidenced claim in the corpus.**

Michael Masterson, chief growth strategist at Agora Publishing, reviewed the top
100 offers of all time and found 91 centred on a single concept — relayed by Alex
Hormozi, who says adopting the rule made his own copy "crisper." The one big idea
must be easy to understand, easy to believe, and interesting or unique. The failure
mode has a name: **toss salad copy**.

Dick Benson reached the same place through direct mail testing: "you can never sell
two things at once." A real example from Garfinkel's co-host: an audiobook sold on
Audible massively outperformed a self-hosted funnel that had to sell the book *and*
a new platform simultaneously.

Harry Dry's version is the most quotable: "the strength of an idea is inversely
proportional to its scope." He cites Hiut Denim — "We make jeans, that's it" — and
extends it to album covers, where the Velvet Underground banana and the Dark Side
of the Moon prism work and Bowie's *Reality* cover, with seven disparate images,
does not.

## 3. Concrete, falsifiable, unownable-by-anyone-else

**CONSENSUS on the principle; Harry Dry supplies the sharpest test.**

**The three-question test.** Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else
say this? Three no's means rubbish; three yes's means you're onto something.
Worked live: BEFORE, a recruitment ad reading "Don't just get a job, change an
entire industry" — fails all three. AFTER, New Balance: "Worn by supermodels in
London and dads in Ohio."

**Why concrete wins.** Citing Lisa Cron — "if I can't see it, it's not there yet."
Read six phrases aloud and people recall the pitbull, the Irishman and the leg of
lamb, never the "seamless transition" or the "better way."

**The zoom-in method.** Write the abstract phrase at the top of a page and keep
rewriting, asking "what do I actually mean?", until you land on a concrete object.
Regain fitness → getting off the couch → running → 5K → **Couch to 5K**, the
most-downloaded fitness program ever, made by a Boston TV producer.

**Why falsifiable matters.** A sentence that can be proved true or false puts the
writer on the chopping block, and that is what makes readers pay attention.

**"You can't talk, you can only point."** Describing someone as having good values
is weak; pointing at checkable facts is strong. Applied to selling gold: don't say
it's a great investment, point at the fifty-year price chart, at what happened in
the last crash, at how much Warren Buffett holds.

**"Never write an ad a competitor can sign"** (Jim Durkin, via Dry). The Volkswagen
line about Volvo's speedometer having six numbers passes because no competitor
could sign it and anyone can verify it.

**Roy Furr's version is arithmetic.** Working an oil-stock promo, he built a
spreadsheet of barrels per share, price per barrel and market cap, and found
investors were "buying more than 40 barrels of oil for less than a dollar a share."
One surprising stat, found by doing maths nobody — including the company — had
bothered to do. "You want to beat the control? Do the math." And: "if your message
is based on incredibly compelling substance, you actually don't need fancy language
at all."

**David Deutsch's route to getting good fast:** sell door-to-door or telemarket to
learn objection-handling live; know the product better than anyone; study Ogilvy,
Caples and Halbert line by line — word count, sentence length, verb-to-adjective
ratio; and get tracked feedback from the market.

## 4. Story over statistics, in the opening

**CONSENSUS, with unusually clean supporting data.**

- Georgi's BioHarmony lead carried zero statistics and hit 70–100 sales a day
  within weeks of launch, with Facebook conversion as high as 6% on the lower price
  point, and a Mercola email blast pulling 5% conversion and a $550 EPC.
- An Agora nonprofit donation test put an emotional single-child story against
  plain hunger statistics: **$2.20 average donation versus $1.00**.
- Sabri Suby: "nothing, and I mean nothing, works better than story-based copy for
  cold traffic audiences."
- Don Hauptman's lift letter for *Cardiac Alert* opened "When I was 16, my father
  died of a heart attack" — the publisher's own story. It was never beaten in
  testing, and was included in every subsequent mailing regardless of who wrote the
  main package.
- Gary Halbert, via Justin: "the number one sin in copy is to be boring."

Hauptman's corollary: **missing dramatic detail kills a true story.** He critiqued
a fundraising letter about a man who fled a communist regime that omitted how he
escaped, what the danger was, and whether he left family behind — what happens when
nobody asks the deeper questions.

## 5. Tease the mechanism, never explain it

**CONSENSUS.**

Kyle Milligan, breaking down the Todd Brown letter: describe the *properties* of
the secret, never the secret itself. "The specificity builds the intrigue."
"Whenever you introduce a mechanism or you introduce a new concept, you cannot just
pivot and start moving on — you've got to tease."

Sabri Suby's version is commercial: revealing the mechanism in generic terms hands
the reader a DIY substitute and kills the need to buy. His fix is to wrap the
mechanism in proprietary framing — a specific growing region, a specific source.

Georgi's mistake 4 is the same instinct from the writer's side: over-explaining is
teaching, and teaching in the lead loses the reader.

Jon Benson's open loops are the mechanical expression of it. Start a story the
prospect cares about, interrupt before resolution, let the subconscious hold the
tension, resolve later for the payoff. He claims up to nine nested loops in a single
VSL, none resolved until the end. Every opened loop must be closed — track them on
paper. To close one, use recall language: "now, remember, and think back to when I
said…" — "remember" forces the brain to search for the referenced information, and
works even if it was never actually said.

## 6. Write to one person and repel the wrong ones

**CONSENSUS.**

Georgi: "you're trying to make it feel like you're having a one-on-one conversation
with the prospect… they care about themselves and what's in it for them."
Anna Hickman: "your sales page is not about you. It is 100% about your customer."

**The disqualifier is a conversion device, not a courtesy.** Jesse Forrest runs a
"join if / don't join if" section — "if you're this type of person, don't join. I
don't want you to join." Anna Hickman pairs "this is for me" (3–5 relatable
problems) with "this is not for you if." The Todd Brown letter carries repeated
disqualifying language right through to the close ("any hesitation, skip it —
you're not ready"). All report the same result: fewer refunds, less support load,
more trust.

**Blair Warren's One Sentence Persuasion**, which Cattoni uses as the bar a hook
must clear: people will do anything for those who encourage their dreams, justify
their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions, and help them throw
rocks at their enemies.

**Kennedy's Miracle Ear example is the sharpest emotional read in the corpus.** The
industry's default creative sold hearing birds again. The real buying emotion is
fear of being labelled senile by your family and shipped into assisted living —
"when the family begins to think they're losing it, what does the family start to
do?" Note this pattern when writing to a successful person who is stuck: the stated
want and the actual fear are rarely the same sentence.

## 7. Proof that can be checked

**CONSENSUS.**

Don Hauptman's investment-newsletter testimonial worked because the man was a
bartender with no pension and no 401k — the specificity is what made it land. A
Canadian restaurant-trade client published testimonial-givers' names, cities *and*
phone numbers; readers concluded he couldn't be making it up. Hauptman reused the
trick, with permission, for a financial conference selling exhibit space.

Jesse Forrest escalates past self-testimony to third-party proof — calling
freelancing "a trillion-dollar industry" with sources — and stacks specific
credibility numbers rather than vague claims: twenty years, hundreds of clients,
103 industries.

Alex Cattoni's placement rule: stack social proof heaviest right before the offer
ask or on the cart page. And the hard line — "only use real social proof. If you're
not, you won't be in business long."

Kyle Milligan's structural version: credibility functions as a proxy for *safety*
in the Nesby model, which is why the Todd Brown letter stacks it before the headline
has even landed.

## 8. Branding versus direct response

**CONTESTED — and the sharpest disagreement in the corpus.**

**Sabri Suby's position.** Most clients "don't even have a brand even though they
think that they have a brand… they have a logo. That's not a brand." His copy is
"colloquial, conversational," aimed at "what are the things that are keeping them up
at night." Brand campaigns "report on sunshine and giggles and impressions. You
cannot take those things to the bank account." His resolution is a test: run the
branded variant alongside the direct-response version and let the data decide, then
ask for exclusive budget when DR wins. He concedes he will run the branded variant
if a client insists — framed as indulging the client.

He also rebuts "no one reads long copy" with his own numbers: his long-form book
landing page averages roughly 20 minutes on site across millions of views. The
people who don't read it were never going to buy. Length isn't the enemy of
conversion; irrelevance is.

**Harry Dry sits on the other side without arguing it directly.** His whole case is
that positioning *is* the lever: Snickers and the discontinued Fuse bar were near
identical products with different copy. Journeyman footballer Dave Kitson had a
worse career than Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard and Owen, and outsold their combined
memoirs by writing an anonymous tell-all as "The Secret Footballer."

**Kennedy's position undercuts both.** He says he never beat a Gary Halbert control
in five attempts, and would rather win on envelope, lift note and list segmentation:
"you always have to come to this with more than message, because message alone
rarely is the answer."

**Suby versus the classic canon.** He credits Schwartz, Halbert and Collier, then
argues their playbooks are insufficient unmodified, because those "greats never had
to compete with the absolute chaos of the internet." Skepticism now starts at 100%,
so the only opening move is to provide value in advance — his "value-based
marketing."

## 9. Where copy comes from

**CONTESTED.**

**Joanna Wiebe: assemble, don't write.** Mine a voice-of-customer library, drag raw
customer quotes into Problem / Agitation / Solution columns, keep only what serves
the one problem this piece solves. Real lines she pulled and used: "I was really
tired of frozen meals," "I've just plain grown tired of the cooking game." Huel
solves many problems including weight loss; she deliberately excludes those to keep
the message singular. "You do not have to include yourself in the process of writing
copy."

Her escalation move: hunt for the moment of highest tension or highest pleasure and
rewrite summary statements into scene-level specificity — "you peer into an open
drawer in your refrigerator and a half-sealed package of baloney looks back at you."

Her verification test: read the copy aloud to someone with their eyes closed and ask
what they pictured. If they saw a kitchen or a brown paper bag that isn't literally
in the text, the copy is under-specific.

Her scope rule: a homepage claim has to be universally true for the whole audience;
a single nurture or cart-abandon email can safely lean into one narrow problem that
wouldn't hold as brand-wide positioning.

**Hickman and Forrest write from instinct and formula.** Both hand-craft example
copy from psychological principles and narrative structure. Directly opposed method,
both with results behind them.

**Harry Dry is opposed to formula from the other direction.** He refuses to give a
fill-in-the-blank template, and instead walks through roughly 25 rewrites of one ad
over two days. "You can only rewrite simply." That sits in tension with
Conversionly's fixed 8-step VSL script and Jon Benson's rules-based NLP triggers —
Dry is diagnostic, they are prescriptive.

## 10. Page and script structures

### Anna Hickman's sales page, as actually built live

Hero (communicate what/who/benefit in under 3 seconds, stop short of answering
everything) → Problem-solving via AIDA → Steps to Success, 3–5 max even if the real
process has 50 → Social proof sprinkled, not blocked → What's Included, benefit-led
bullets → Purchase Options, 5 bullets max per package → About, only insofar as it
links to shared struggle → This Is For Me → This Is Not For You If → FAQ → Final CTA
as a yes/no question (the "question behaviour effect," so the reader owns the
decision).

Her CTA button rules: be specific, never "learn more"; frame around what they get,
not what they give; write in first person — "plan my workout," not "plan your
workout." Her live hero headline: "a workout plan you can actually stick to. Yes,
even you."

### Jesse Forrest's sales page, dissecting his own

Headline (benefit-stacked) + sub-headline (kills the primary objection) → Video →
CTA above the fold → Desire questions → Relatable problems → Empathy story
(Before → Spark → Result) → Opportunity and credibility backup → Product intro +
CTA → Module breakdown → Bonuses → "Why listen to me" → No-brainer bonus priced
*above* the core offer → Qualification (join if / don't join if) → CTA →
Testimonials → Offer repeated → FAQ → Earnings disclaimer.

Assume ~20% read every word and 80% skim — but almost all buyers eventually read in
full before purchasing, which is why long pages still work. Video lifts conversion
only if it summarises the whole page's argument, and it proves you're a real person.

### The Todd Brown "leaked recordings" letter — 21 moves

The single most valuable structural artefact in the corpus, walked through by Kyle
Milligan. The letter drove a Wealth Press launch clearing an estimated $800,000 in
a few days against a prior in-house record of $330,000.

1. Eyebrow/deck stacks Only + credibility before the headline lands.
2. Headline **expands** the eyebrow rather than pattern-interrupting to a new idea.
3. Mechanism introduced then teased — properties described, secret withheld.
4. Credibility stacked immediately, functioning as a proxy for safety.
5. Escalates past reader experience: "even if you consider yourself a pro" — kills
   the "I've seen this already" objection.
6. Names the guru, hits Big + Fast.
7. Drills to the true mechanism by repeating one phrase verbatim — repetition is the
   signal for which mechanism to track.
8. More credibility: new + big + safe in a single line.
9. Easy/Anybody section — a non-native English speaker "forced to find a simpler
   method," killing the objection that expertise takes years. Framed as
   *discovered*, not invented, which lowers sales resistance.
10. The big promise repeated relentlessly across the first several pages.
11. Third-party endorsement from the founder.
12. The ask minimised: "just one 35-minute secret meeting each week."
13. Transition to offer copy at roughly 70% desire / 30% offer.
14. A "flash roll" of insider detail, immediately defused with reassurance.
15. The Catalyst — why access is possible at all despite the exclusivity.
16. Scarcity (limited passes), explicitly not urgency. Reframes the purchase as
    claiming a pass rather than paying a fee.
17. First price anchor, paired with a soft disqualifier.
18. Price-drop narrative, told as the seller fighting for the reader.
19. Three bonuses, each following promise → proof → promise restated.
20. Close stacking urgency and scarcity with repeated disqualifying language.
21. P.S. restating core promise and exclusivity only, no new claims — built for
    skimmers who jump from subheads to the end.

**Milligan's own critique:** the recap order is backwards. It should run price
anchor → bonuses and testimonials → value stack built to *exceed* the anchor →
*then* the price-cut narrative → close. Doing the cut before the stack deflates the
reveal.

**The biggest offer-copy mistake at every skill level:** writers *explain* the
deliverables instead of *selling* them. "We don't explain what to expect, we sell
what to expect." Run the same machinery over the offer section that you ran over the
lead.

### Conversionly's 8-step VSL

Big promise centred on the transformation, never the mechanism → social proof
immediately after → twist the knife (contrast their after with the viewer's before)
→ solution in about three bite-sized chunks, withholding enough to justify a call →
"imagine this" → first CTA plus a demo of the literal next steps → second CTA →
outro.

Example hook: "in the next couple of minutes I'm gonna go over how you can stack
your calendar with qualified sales appointments" — the transformation (appointments)
rather than the mechanism (cold email, ads, mailers).

**VSL length has compressed.** They used to run 20, 15 and 10 minutes; now under 5,
with a longer deep dive — their example runs an hour and fifteen — on the
post-booking thank-you page, to qualify and disqualify further before the call.

### Daniel Priestley's assessment funnel — the outlier

The only source not building a persuade-and-close page. He defers the sale entirely
to post-quiz follow-up.

Landing page: hook (either a frustration hook or a results/readiness question) →
subheading promising X questions → value proposition naming three areas measured →
credibility (who you are, background, research) → CTA. The CTA combination — "start
the quiz, it only takes 3 minutes, it's completely free, you get immediate
recommendations" — gets 20–40% of visitors to start.

Quiz: contact capture (name and email mandatory, location automatic, phone optional)
→ 10 yes/no best-practice questions generating the score → 5 qualifying questions
(current situation; desired outcome in 90 days; biggest obstacle and what's been
tried; preferred solution type, used as a budget proxy; an open box) → dynamic
results page with a visual score, a big reveal, three insights and tiered next
steps.

He discloses that he co-founded the tool he's demonstrating.

## 11. Formulas, checklists and named frameworks

**Roy Furr's Sequence of Beliefs**, built on Clayton Makepeace's founding question —
"what do I, your prospect, have to believe to be true before I'll be interested in
your offer?" Attention: this is worth your time; I'm a credible authority; I can
offer a desired outcome. Interest and desire: my promise is proven; your objections
are addressed; you can let go of your limiting beliefs. Action: this is incredible
value; you can act today risk-free; you must act now.

**Dan Kennedy's six-belief speech structure** from *Speak to Sell*, which sold
around $150 million from the stage using essentially the same speech for nine or ten
years: what you're doing now is frustrating and costly → it's not your fault,
everyone who led you here is an idiot or a charlatan → there is a better way, a
three-part formula of message, market and media → you need a compelling message with
a USP and a call to action → target ideal prospects → use media efficiently.

**Nesby** (Kyle Milligan) — New, Easy, Safe, Big, each with an alter ego for when the
direct claim is hard: New↔Only, Easy↔Anybody, Safe↔Predictable, Big↔Fast. Used as a
line-by-line audit: "I could take an entire box of their copy, draw a box around it,
say just delete this — none of it has new, safe, easy, or big in there."

Copy Squad's related model adds the two promo types — a **system** (a step-by-step
process) or a **prediction** (a forecast to act on) — and the pattern move for
Safe: you can't prove a prediction, but you can prove it fits a repeating pattern.
"You must create the pattern. You must find as many examples that re-demonstrate,
and re-demonstrate, and re-demonstrate that pattern."

**Roy Furr's ABC headline test** — Audience, Benefit, Curiosity. His three big idea
types: urgent problem, 10x opportunity, imminent prediction.

**Gary Bencivenga's Persuasion Equation** — urgent problem + unique promise +
unquestionable proof + user-friendly proposition. Used in the *Game of Conversions*
breakdown of John Caples's 1929 Phoenix Mutual ad, "To men who want to quit work
someday." Caples added "someday" deliberately, vague on timeframe, to widen the
qualifying audience; the ad's middle section had originally run as a standalone ad
before being folded in.

**The 4 U's** — Urgent, Unique, Useful, Ultra-specific (Bob Bly, via Jon Benson).
Benson reports the missing urgency element alone lifted conversion a full point on a
$469 offer converting around 5%; the headline in question was drafted ~85–90% by
ChatGPT and then hand-checked against the four U's in about fifteen minutes.

His **reverse benefits** idea: phrase some benefits as pain removed rather than gain
added — "no copy skills required" — because "we live mostly in a Negative Zone."

**Cattoni's 9-component sales offer** — Headline · S-Hook (Story, Science or
Supposition) · Problem · Offer · Benefits · Social Proof · Scarcity · Risk Reversal ·
one CTA, asked repeatedly and never diluted. Her five hooks: Threat/Warning/Pain
(for a problem-aware audience), Education/FYI, Confirm Beliefs, FOMO, Relevancy.

Her formatting rules: three headlines maximum, sized and staged; paragraphs capped
at three sentences; coherence markers between them; Hemingway grade-8 readability;
check mobile by narrowing desktop margins. And the CTA sandwich — above the fold,
at checkpoints through the body, an order box after the social proof and value
stack, and always one at the very bottom, "because so often the very first thing
that people do when they decide it's time to buy is scroll right to the bottom."

**Adam Erhart's 15 psychological triggers** — halo effect, serial position, recency,
mere exposure, loss aversion, compromise effect, anchoring, choice overload, framing
effect, IKEA effect, Pygmalion effect, confirmation bias, zero-risk bias, bandwagon,
and blind-spot bias, which is the observation that all the others still work on
people who have been told about them.

**Shweta Saxena's line-level hooks** — "because…" (citing the photocopier compliance
study, roughly 60% to 93% compliance from a near-content-free reason), "do you
know…", "here's the thing", "according to a study", "as discussed", and the loop.
She's the only source who warns that her own technique backfires when overused.

**John Adair's creativity method**, via Garfinkel: draw analogies from nature (radar
from bat echolocation, the magnifying glass from dew on leaves); make the strange
familiar and the familiar strange; use your unconscious and accept it won't hit your
deadline; widen your span of relevance (the ballpoint pen invented by a sculptor,
the parking meter by a journalist, pneumatic tyres by a vet, the seed drill by a
farmer-organist noticing organ-pipe spacing). And "Always Be Asking" — Claude
Hopkins pestered Schlitz managers until he learned they steam-washed their bottles
four times, then used it to take Schlitz from fifteenth to first.

## 12. Testing, and what actually moves the number

**Dick Benson's seven rules**, reconstructed from his book and archival clippings:

1. Test packages from different writers, never the same writer twice — different
   writers have irreducibly different frames of reference.
2. Test bonuses, and they needn't relate to the core offer.
3. Write powerful copy for the whole list, don't niche-segment.
4. A two-time buyer is twice as likely to buy as a one-time buyer.
5. You can never sell two things at once.
6. Dollar for dollar, bonuses beat cash discounts.
7. Exception to 6: an exclusive reduced price to a house list will more than pay its
   way — framed as a friends-and-family favour, not a commodity discount.

**Rule 2 is counter-intuitive and tested.** Benson's related "real-age"
self-assessment bonus, offered against the *UC Wellness Letter* control across three
lists, **dropped conversions 20% in all three**. "They didn't want to know."
Meanwhile unrelated premiums lift: a BMW dealership's bottle of wine with every car
sale; an *Advertising Age* coffee mug; Omaha Steaks' calculator; and on a $40M
Abacus Travel letter, a box of Godiva chocolates for attending the sales meeting was
what actually got people through the door.

**Kennedy contradicts rule 3 directly.** He splits a financial list by occupation —
doctors, dentists, lawyers and private pilots make up "60 to 70 percent" of a
typical list — and mails up to seven package versions, each customised in language
and testimonial. Two testers, real data, opposite conclusions.

**Kennedy's other tested findings.** The Jeff Paul "$4,000 a Day in Your Underwear"
ad got a 20% response lift in conservative magazines and about 10% elsewhere after
adding an unrelated political paragraph — positioning against the reader's named
enemy moves response independently of the offer. And lapsed subscribers can
outperform recent buyers, because they still believe the underlying idea, just not
guru number one — yet brokers price hotline names at a premium.

**On unlimited anything:** "anything you give away unlimited, you just shot yourself
in the foot, because it has no value." A capped offer reads as worth more.

## 13. Selling to coaches and consultants specifically

**The niche is not saturated.** Megan Kachigan: "there's a ton of coaches and course
creators… but come on in, it's not saturated… it means there is a great need for
it." Visible competition reads as evidence of a growing market.

**Client work is won through relationships, not cold pitching.** Her channels:
Facebook groups, LinkedIn, Instagram, masterminds, networking calls, conferences.
Referrals arrive with pre-built trust and produce better clients.

**Copywriters lose these clients by going quiet, not by writing badly.** Her field
data from client complaints: "ghosted me," "didn't really deliver," "didn't make
edits." The fix is a written scope, timeline and payment contract plus plain
responsiveness. Reliability "can't be taught."

**Consulting can beat a delivered letter.** Ryan Moran's framing of Lawton Chiles:
rather than paying five or ten thousand for a sales letter, work hand-in-hand so
the client's own skill compounds. The headline-only change on Adam Spiel's landing
page lifted conversions 170% and produced $25,000 in seven days.

**Lead with the transformation, not the mechanism** (Conversionly) — the single most
transferable rule from the VSL material to coaching offers.

## 14. Integrity — recorded, not recommended

Part of the Kennedy-era canon in this corpus is deceptive and, under current
consumer law, legally exposed. It is recorded because it is historically
load-bearing and because you will meet it in the wild. Do not execute from it.

- **Manufactured scarcity and urgency.** Kennedy's beat-the-control list explicitly
  includes adding fake scarcity limits, arbitrary per-household caps, and call-in
  windows split by surname or geography with no justification. The honest version of
  the same lever is a real capacity, a real deadline, or a real cohort limit.
  Milligan's distinction — "urgency is time, scarcity is quantity" — still holds; the
  constraint just has to be true.
- **Celebrity implication.** Paying for an unattributed quote, or paying a
  celebrity's relative to imply association, is misrepresentation.
- **Invented stories.** Georgi and Justin describe truthfulness as niche-dependent:
  invented stories treated as fair game in dating, legally dangerous in health. Their
  own stated practice is to dramatise real accounts rather than invent them. That is

## What the experts agree on

1. **The lead does the work, and it is the highest-leverage thing you can rewrite.**
   The lead is the first 2–3 pages of a letter or the first 1–2 minutes of a VSL.
   Stefan Georgi beat a V-Shred control by 72% changing only the lead, moving the
   offer from 10–20 sales a day to roughly 1,500. He was paid $63,000 for one
   two-page lead rewrite at $3,000 per point of lift, and says he would "100% test
   new leads before new big ideas." Roy Furr agrees on structure: hook is the idea,
   headline buys readership, lead pulls deeper while withholding the payoff.

2. **One idea. Not nineteen.** Michael Masterson reviewed the top 100 offers of all
   time at Agora and found 91 ran on a single concept (via Hormozi). Dick Benson's
   testing rule 5 is "you can never sell two things at once." Harry Dry: "the
   strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope." Every sub-bullet,
   story and fact exists to reinforce the one idea. The failure mode has a name —
   toss salad copy.

3. **Concrete and falsifiable beats abstract and safe.** Harry Dry's three-question
   test: can I visualize it, can I falsify it, can nobody else say this. Roy Furr:
   "you want to beat the control? Do the math" — he found a single stat (investors
   buying 40 barrels of oil for under a dollar a share) that carried a whole promo.
   David Deutsch: study copy down to word count and verb-to-adjective ratio.
   Vague, unfalsifiable claims are the thing every source in this corpus attacks.

4. **Story beats statistics in the opening, and the data is not close.** Georgi's
   BioHarmony lead carried zero statistics and hit 70–100 sales a day within weeks.
   An Agora nonprofit test put an emotional single-child story against plain hunger
   statistics: $2.20 average donation versus $1.00. Sabri Suby: "nothing, and I mean
   nothing, works better than story-based copy for cold traffic audiences." Stats
   belong later in the body, sparingly, once the emotional hook has landed.

5. **Tease the mechanism, never explain it.** Georgi's fourth lead mistake is being
   a teacher instead of a marketer — keep it "battery enzyme," not "NP241."
   Kyle Milligan on the Todd Brown letter: describe the *properties* of the secret,
   never the secret. "The specificity builds the intrigue." Sabri Suby's version is
   that revealing the mechanism hands the reader a DIY substitute and kills the sale.

6. **Write to one person, and point at facts rather than describing yourself.**
   Georgi: "you're trying to make it feel like you're having a one-on-one
   conversation with the prospect," and his third named mistake is Ron Burgundy
   syndrome — first-person story with no relation back to the reader. Harry Dry's
   version: "you can't talk, you can only point." Anna Hickman: "your sales page is
   not about you. It is 100% about your customer."

7. **Repel the wrong buyer on purpose.** Jesse Forrest runs a "join if / don't join
   if" qualification section — "if you're this type of person, don't join." Anna
   Hickman pairs "this is for me" with "this is not for you if." The Todd Brown
   letter carries repeated disqualifying language right through the close. All three
   report the same reasons: fewer refunds, less support load, more trust.

8. **Proof must be specific enough to be checkable.** Don Hauptman's investment
   testimonial landed because the man was a bartender with no pension. A Canadian
   client published testimonial-givers' names, cities and phone numbers — readers
   concluded he couldn't be making it up. Alex Cattoni: "only use real social proof.
   If you're not, you won't be in business long."

## Named frameworks & methods

**Georgi's 5 lead mistakes** — 1 burying the lead / throat clearing · 2 leaning on
broad statistics · 3 not talking to the prospect (Ron Burgundy syndrome) ·
4 over-explaining, being a teacher instead of a marketer · 5 forgetting to
fascinate. Fix for 5: stack 3–5 curiosity bullets near the end of the lead, each
posing an unanswered riddle. Source material he names: the *Screaming Eagle*
newsletter and its ~21 categories of curiosity bullet.

**Roy Furr's Sequence of Beliefs** — built on Clayton Makepeace's founding question,
"what do I, your prospect, have to believe to be true before I'll be interested in
your offer?" Attention: 1 this is worth your time · 2 I'm a credible authority ·
3 I can offer a desired outcome, emotionally and practically. Interest and desire:
4 my promise is proven and believable · 5 your objections are addressed · 6 you can
let go of your limiting beliefs. Action: 7 this is incredible value · 8 you can act
today risk-free · 9 you must act now.

**Dan Kennedy's 6-belief speech structure** (*Speak to Sell*, ~$150M sold from the
stage) — 1 what you're doing now is frustrating and costly · 2 it's not your fault,
everyone who led you here is an idiot or a charlatan · 3 there is a better way, a
three-part formula: message, market, media · 4 you need a compelling message with a
USP and a call to action · 5 target ideal prospects, don't mass-market · 6 use media
to deliver efficiently.

**Roy Furr's ABC headline test** — Audience, Benefit, Curiosity. And his three big
idea types: urgent problem, 10x opportunity, imminent prediction.

**Harry Dry's three-question test** — can I visualize it, can I falsify it, can
nobody else say this. Plus the zoom-in method: write the abstract phrase at the top
of a page and keep asking "what do I actually mean?" until you hit a concrete
object. Worked example: regain fitness → getting off the couch → running → 5K →
**Couch to 5K**.

**Nesby / the four pillars** — New, Easy, Safe, Big, each with an alter ego for when
the direct claim is hard to make: New↔Only, Easy↔Anybody, Safe↔Predictable,
Big↔Fast. Kyle Milligan uses it as a line-by-line audit, not a headline formula:
any block of copy with none of the four in it should be deleted.

**The Rule of One** — Masterson via Hormozi. The one big idea must be easy to
understand, easy to believe, and interesting or unique.

**Gary Bencivenga's Persuasion Equation** — urgent problem + unique promise +
unquestionable proof + user-friendly proposition.

**The 4 U's** — Urgent, Unique, Useful, Ultra-specific (Bob Bly, via Jon Benson).
Benson reports adding the missing urgency element alone lifted conversion a full
point on a $469 offer.

**Dick Benson's 7 testing rules** — 1 test packages from different writers, never
the same writer twice · 2 test bonuses, they needn't relate to the offer · 3 write
powerful copy for the whole list, don't niche-segment · 4 a two-time buyer is twice
as likely to buy · 5 you can never sell two things at once · 6 dollar for dollar,
bonuses beat discounts · 7 exception: an exclusive reduced price to a house list
pays its way.

**Cattoni's 9-component sales offer** — Headline · S-Hook (Story, Science or
Supposition) · Problem · Offer · Benefits · Social Proof · Scarcity · Risk Reversal ·
one CTA. Her five hook types: Threat/Warning/Pain, Education/FYI, Confirm Beliefs,
FOMO, Relevancy.

**Conversionly's 8-step VSL** — big promise centred on the transformation not the
mechanism · social proof immediately after · twist the knife · solution in ~3
bite-sized chunks · imagine this · first CTA plus a demo of the literal next steps ·
second CTA · outro.

**Jon Benson's open loops** — start a story they care about, interrupt before
resolution, let the subconscious hold tension, resolve later. Loops can nest;
every one must be closed, tracked on paper. Close with recall language ("remember,
think back to when I said…") then restate and resolve.

**Wiebe's assemble-don't-write method** — mine a voice-of-customer library, sort raw
quotes into Problem / Agitation / Solution, keep only what serves the one target
problem, escalate generic lines into sensory scenes. Verification: read it aloud to
someone with their eyes closed and ask what they pictured. If they saw something
that isn't literally in the text, the copy is under-specific.

**Full page skeletons** — Anna Hickman's 11-section and Jesse Forrest's 18-section
sales pages, and the complete 21-move walkthrough of the Todd Brown letter, are in
`references/synthesis.md`.

## Contrarian / disputed takes

**Branding versus direct response.** Sabri Suby is the sharpest voice in the corpus:
most clients "don't even have a brand… they have a logo." Brand campaigns "report on
sunshine and giggles and impressions. You cannot take those things to the bank
account." His method is to run a branded variant against the DR version and let the
data decide. Note he concedes he'll run the branded variant if a client insists.
Harry Dry sits on the other side without arguing the point directly — his whole case
is that positioning and craft *are* the lever (Snickers versus the discontinued Fuse
bar; a journeyman footballer outselling Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard and Owen combined
by writing anonymously).

**Craft-led versus formula-led.** Harry Dry is anti-formula — ~25 messy rewrites of
one ad over two days, "you can only rewrite simply." That sits in direct tension with
Conversionly's fixed 8-step VSL script and Jon Benson's rules-based NLP triggers.
Dry is diagnostic (tests applied after the fact); the others are prescriptive.

**Where the copy comes from.** Joanna Wiebe insists "you do not have to include
yourself in the process of writing copy" — assemble from customer language. Hickman
and Forrest both hand-craft from narrative instinct. Genuinely opposed methods, both
with results behind them.

**Whether copy is even the lever.** Kennedy says he never beat a Halbert control in
five attempts and would rather win on envelope, lift note and list segmentation:
"you always have to come to this with more than message, because message alone
rarely is the answer." That cuts against the entire premise of most copy training.

**Segmentation.** Dick Benson says write powerful copy for the whole list and don't
niche-segment. Kennedy mails up to 7 occupation-customised versions of the same
package. Directly opposed, both from testers with real data.

**Bonuses.** Benson's tested finding is that bonuses needn't relate to the offer —
his related "real-age" bonus dropped conversions 20% across three lists, while
unrelated premiums (a mug, a calculator, Godiva chocolates) lifted response. Cuts
against the instinct to tie every bonus back to the core benefit.

**Question-based leads.** Georgi names Craig Clemens using them successfully, then
says he avoids them himself. A stylistic disagreement between two commercially
successful writers, not a settled rule.

**Stats in the lead.** Standard practice in health and financial DR; Georgi calls it
a conversion killer. Unresolved, but the story-over-stats evidence in this corpus is
one-sided.

### Integrity note — material recorded but NOT recommended

Part of the Kennedy-era tactical canon in this corpus is deceptive and, under
current consumer law, legally exposed. It is recorded because it is historically
load-bearing and because you will meet it in the wild. Do not execute from it.

- **Manufactured scarcity and urgency.** Kennedy's beat-the-control list includes
  "add fake scarcity limits," arbitrary per-household caps and call-in windows split
  by surname or geography with no real justification. The honest version of the same
  lever is genuine capacity, a real deadline, or a real cohort limit — Kyle
  Milligan's "urgency is time, scarcity is quantity" distinction still applies, but
  the constraint has to be true.
- **Celebrity implication.** Paying for an unattributed quote, or paying a
  celebrity's relative to imply association, is misrepresentation.
- **Invented stories.** Georgi and Justin describe truthfulness as niche-dependent —
  invented stories treated as fair game in dating, legally dangerous in health.
  Their own stated practice is to dramatise real accounts rather than invent them.
  That is the defensible line: dramatise what happened, never manufacture it.
- **Irrational value stacks.** The Cheese Lovers Club justified $39.95/month against
  the ~$820,000 cost of flying to every source country. Kennedy calls it ludicrous
  himself; it ran as their control for five years. Anchoring on a real comparable is
  the version that survives scrutiny.

## Execution playbook

**IF you have one thing to fix and limited time, THEN rewrite the lead, not the
offer or the big idea** (per Georgi — 72% control beat from the lead alone, and he
tests leads before big ideas because the effort-to-payoff ratio is better).

**IF the copy opens with throat clearing, THEN delete every sentence before the line
that actually captures attention** (Georgi, mistake 1).

**IF a line can't be visualized, can't be falsified, or a competitor could sign it,
THEN it is not finished** (Harry Dry's three-question test; Jim Durkin's "never write
an ad a competitor can sign").

**IF you are tempted to open with a statistic, THEN move it to the body and open with
a story instead** (Georgi mistake 2; the Agora $2.20 vs $1.00 donation test; Suby on
cold traffic).

**IF you have introduced a mechanism, THEN tease its properties and stop — do not
explain it** (Milligan: "the specificity builds the intrigue"; Georgi mistake 4).

**IF the copy is carrying more than one selling proposition, THEN cut back to one**
(Rule of One; Benson's law of one; Dry on scope).

**IF the reader could be a bad fit, THEN say so explicitly in the copy** (Forrest's
join-if/don't-join-if; Hickman's "this is not for you if").

**IF you are writing for a coaching or consulting offer specifically, THEN lead with
the transformation, not the mechanism** (Conversionly), and expect the proof burden
to sit on named, checkable people rather than on product specs (Hauptman).

**IF you are about to claim scarcity or urgency, THEN verify the constraint is real
before you write it.** See the integrity note above.

### Default numbers the experts actually use

- **Lead length** — first 2–3 pages of a letter; first 1–2 minutes of a VSL.
- **Desire-to-offer split** — roughly 70/30 (Milligan, on the Todd Brown letter).
- **Fascination bullets in a lead** — 3–5, each an unanswered riddle (Georgi).
- **CTA repeats on a sales page** — minimum 3: above the fold, mid-page after the
  product intro, and at the very bottom (Forrest). Cattoni adds an order box after
  the social proof and value stack.
- **Paragraph length** — 3 sentences max (Cattoni); 2 lines max (Harry Dry, self-
  imposed since March 2021).
- **Reading level** — 7th–8th grade, checked in Hemingway (Cattoni). Fifth grade
  (Hickman).
- **Steps in a "how it works" section** — 3–5 max, even if the real process has 50
  (Hickman).
- **VSL length** — under 5 minutes now, with a longer deep-dive on the post-booking
  thank-you page (Conversionly). The old 10/15/20-minute norm is gone.
- **Quiz/assessment landing page** — 20–40% of visitors start it when the CTA states
  time-to-complete, that it's free, and that recommendations are immediate
  (Priestley).
- **Skim rate** — assume ~20% read every word, 80% skim (Forrest). Build for both.

### Pre-flight checklist

1. Can I name the ONE idea in a single sentence?
2. Does the lead open on the attention line, with nothing before it?
3. Is there a story in the first 2–3 pages, and are the statistics out of them?
4. Is the mechanism teased and unexplained?
5. Does every claim pass visualize / falsify / nobody-else-can-say-it?
6. Is the proof specific enough to be checked — occupation, city, number, chart?
7. Is there an explicit disqualifier for the wrong buyer?
8. Are all scarcity and urgency claims literally true?
9. Are the CTAs above the fold, mid-page and at the bottom, all asking for one thing?
10. Read aloud to someone with their eyes closed — did they picture what's on the page?

### Failure modes

- **Throat clearing.** Fix: cut to the attention line.
- **Toss salad copy** — many selling propositions, none dominant. Fix: pick one,
  demote the rest to supporting evidence.
- **Ron Burgundy syndrome** — an "I" story that never returns to the reader. Fix:
  address "you" early and often; in third-person stories use "imagine you're…".
- **Teaching instead of selling** — mechanism names, biology, citations up front.
  Fix: defer depth until curiosity is banked.
- **Explaining the deliverables instead of selling them.** Milligan names this as the
  most common offer-copy mistake at every skill level. Fix: run the same Nesby
  machinery over the offer section that you ran over the lead.
- **Unfalsifiable claims.** Fix: replace with a fact, a chart, a number, a comparison.
